# Competitions:
There are a few competitions to choose from when you want to get into competitive robotics. What you choose is dependent on a lot of factors, such as time, experience, goals, and money (Robotics is not a cheap sport especially at higher levels). You also need to consider the communities and companies behind each competition. This factor is most notable with FIRST (For Inspiration and Recognition of Science and Technology), where communities, reputation, and resources often come somewhat out of the box.

The first three programs I list are youth programs that you may find of interest in mentoring or volunteering for. 

## FIRST Lego League Challenge (FLL)
FLL is a competition that I competed in for around 5 years. It is an interesting competition where robotics is only one part of the competition. The competition is split into four parts:
- Robot competition: Robots compete in 2-minute 30-second rounds, completing tasks on a game mat.
- Robot design: Teams present their robots and explain their design to a panel of judges, where they are judged on various criteria such as how they present and understand their robot, how well they took advantage of their resources, and whether they came into the competition with a good strategy. If you are curious about what robot design judging looks like, I found a [tutorial that includes the rubrics that you may find interesting](https://flltutorials.com/translations/en-us/RobotGame/RobotDesignJudging.pdf) 
- Innovation project: During the season, teams have to identify a problem in the world and come up with a potential engineering solution to solve it. These solutions are judged based on various criteria, such as how well they identified and approached the problem. [Once again, here is a tutorial explaining how the project is judged](https://flltutorials.com/translations/en-us/Project/IPJudging.pdf)
- Core values: Finally, there are core values. This is a judgment of how well a team adheres to the FIRST core values of Discovery, Teamwork, Innovation, and Fun. Unlike the other categories, this is not judged in isolation, but rather is determined based on data collected from the other three parts of the competition. [If you are curious, here is another tutorial](https://flltutorials.com/translations/en-us/CoreValues/CVJudging.pdf)
Given that this competition is meant for those between the ages of 9 and 16, it is unlikely that anyone reading this will be able to compete. However, the program requires volunteers and mentors to run the competitions and lead the various teams. If you want to give volunteering at youth robotics competitions a try without investing a large amount of time, I would recommend starting with FLL, since FLL competitions typically only last one day. I had the opportunity to judge at a recent FLL event, and I can say that it was a very fun experience (it was also nice to be the one asking the questions for the first time).

FLL is also the cheapest competition in the FIRST lineup, and I believe it also has the lowest time commitment. As a result, it is an easier competition to get into than some of the other ones I will be talking about.

It has been a while since I have been heavily involved in the program, but I do have some useful resources:
[FLL tutorials](https://flltutorials.com/en/index.html) is a neat site I found while researching for this post that seems to have all the information one could ask for about how to succeed in FLL.
If you are in Idaho and want help finding local resources, the local FLL regional coordinator, Suzie Steiner, is possibly the best resource to get started with. She can be contacted through suzie@openlabidaho.org. 

## FIRST Tech Challenge (FTC)
I admittedly do not know much about FTC, but it would be criminal not to mention it here.

FTC is a competition where teams build 18x18x18 inch (max size) robots to complete various tasks set at the beginning of the season. Each match lasts 2 minutes and 30 seconds, with the robot acting completely autonomously for the first 30 seconds. It is an interesting competition with a lot of very complex machines packed inside small packages. In terms of the complexity-to-size ratio, I would say that this is probably the most impressive competition at the highest levels (though Rival might take that crown in the future). 

It has an age range of 12-18 years old. While I have not interacted with them, it does look like it would be fun to mentor or to help with an event.

As mentioned above, I have very little experience with this competition due to the fact that I have never competed or volunteered in it. However, I do still know of some resources.
I have heard good things about the [FTC discord server](https://discord.gg/ftc)
Suzie Steiner is also the regional coordinator for FTC in Idaho. If you are in Idaho and have any questions, she can be reached at suzie@openlabidaho.org.

## FIRST Robotics Competition (FRC)
Out of all the competitions listed here, FRC is the one closest to my heart. FRC is where I learned to code and lit the spark of in-depth technical work in me. It is why I hope to make robotics my career, and it acted as motivation for me for the 3.5 years I was a participant.

In FRC, teams build 130-pound (max weight) robots to compete each year. These robots are designed, built, and programmed by the teams over the course of about 3 months before competitions start. From here, the teams will compete in qualifying competitions over another six weeks; finally, teams that qualify compete at the FIRST World Championship. At competitions, the robots compete in 2-minute and 30-second rounds, with the robots acting autonomously for the first 15 seconds of the match and being controlled by drivers for the remainder of the match. 

FRC is amazing because there are resources to go incredibly deep into the competition. 

From a software perspective, many teams are building full custom control algorithms, localization systems, scouting apps, pathing software, and a lot more. 
From a hardware perspective, the robots are massive and typically require a lot of custom work in order to even get them moving.

FRC's award system is also interesting. Most awards are judged in the pits by roaming judges. One award in particular stands out; the Impact Award is considered the highest honor in FRC, higher even than winning the competition. It celebrates teams that have had an "impact" on their community and have made STEM available to more people. In 2024, my team won the Impact award at the Idaho Regional Competition and were given the opportunity to compete at the FIRST World Championship.

The World Championship is an insane event where you get to meet and talk to the best teams in the game. It is a massive learning experience and a chance to see why some of the best teams are considered the best. If you ever get an opportunity to go, I would highly recommend it purely because it is one of the coolest events in the country.

As with the last two competitions, the two most likely ways for a reader of this post to join are either to become a mentor or to volunteer at an event. Both of these tasks are large time commitments with seasons due to the competition's difficulty and the fact that events typically last 2-3 days. However, both are very rewarding. I myself plan to volunteer at the Idaho Regional this year (assuming my class schedule lets me) as a Control System Advisor. Thanks to the size of the event, there are roles for anyone who is interested, from scoring to inspection, team assistance, and field care.

FRC is the most expensive of the FIRST Competitions and potentially the most expensive on this list. Teams typically rely on sponsorships from companies and schools to pay for competition.

Thanks to its tight-knit and somewhat rabid community, FRC has a list of resources so long that I could not hope to cover all of it here. So I will just try to list the most notable ones:
The [Chief Delphi forum](https://www.chiefdelphi.com/) is the main place where FRC people talk about the conversation and share their projects. If you have a question, there is a good chance Chief Delphi already has an answer. It is also host to an overwhelming amount of community resources from the Open Alliance (a group of teams that voluntarily release posts detailing their progress during the season), Code and CAD releases, tools to help teams, and advice covering a very wide range of situations (it's basically the Stack Overflow of FRC).

[The Blue Alliance](https://www.thebluealliance.com/) is a tool used by teams to watch matches from other teams. If you want to look up a team to get a photo of their robot and videos of any matches they have played, this is the place.

[Statbotics](https://www.statbotics.io/) fills a similar role to the Blue Alliance, but with the addition of a statistical model that tries to track how many points worth of value a team brings to a given match. It is a useful tool to see which the best teams are at a specific event. You can also use it to see which teams are doing the best globally, which is often useful as you can use them for sources of inspiration (though the top 20 teams generally consist of a few very established "elite" teams.)

[The FRC discord server](https://discord.gg/frc) is a nice place to get questions answered and to talk to other FRC students, mentors, and alumni in a less rigid fashion. However, they do require you to be involved in FIRST in some way in order to join.

Suzie Steiner strikes again. As one of the founders of the FRC team 5461 VERN (The team I competed on), she is an excellent resource if you are curious about FRC. Here is her email one more time for consistency suzie@openlabidaho.org.

## Rival Robotics
Rival Robotics is a young competition that offered an experience like VEX or FTC, but without age or hardware limitations. This was very interesting, as once you leave the realm of FIRST and universities, it is surprisingly hard to find a competitive robotics experience that offers the unique kind of competition that FIRST offers.

However, on Feb 10th 2026, Rival announced that they would be temporarily halting operations while they focus on their core business of making brushless motors. 

I DMed Amit, one of Rival’s founders and the one who delivered the news shortly after the announcement, to get some more information on Rival’s future. He stated that in a best-case scenario, we might see news in four to six months. He also stated that we will be seeing a completely new competition format. While the new competition format is still in the ideas stage, Amit stated “Basically we're moving away from the VEX Robotics format, and trying to move to something more like Formula 1!” He further clarified that this meant “competing using a standard system”, kinda like how F1 cars look fairly similar but are very different in the details. He also did not confirm whether the no-age-limit and no-hardware-limit policies, saying, “Not sure about the other limits yet. We're overhauling a lot, hopefully for the better.” When asked about potential cost concerns with a standard system, Amit replied, “In an ideal universe, we would fund eligible teams, or people would raise sponsor money.”

It is worth keeping in mind that all of this is in the air right now, and no one is entirely certain what the future holds. In a few months, the above may be completely obsolete information. I will try to keep this article up to date, but the best place to get news is probably the Discord server (linked below).

Ultimately, despite the hiatus, Rival is still a promising competition with a dedicated community eagerly awaiting their next moves. In my personal opinion, I would recommend looking at the competition and keeping up with updates, but would not recommend investing in hardware yet due to the uncertainty currently surrounding the competition. If I remember to, I will update this section once we have more news. 

You can keep track of Rival news and interact with the community on the [Rival Robotics Discord server](https://discord.gg/A98AeUthMk).


## LunaBotics
NASA's Lunabotics challenge is a university-level competition where teams build an "off-world construction bot".

I do not know much about this competition, but I do plan to compete in it with BSU's team once I am enrolled there. If you are attending or plan to attend BSU, this may be worth looking into. This is probably the most technically advanced of all the competitions here. Last I heard, the BSU LunaBotics team was making a SLAM localization system for their robot (which I hope to write about in a future post).

## Idaho Combat Robotics League (ICRL)
ICRL is the local combat robotics league. 

In a nutshell, this is combat robotics: two robots fight until one is unable to move, taps out, or time runs out. If you are familiar with battle bots, this is basically that much on a much smaller (and cheaper) scale. They have arenas for ant weight (1 pound) and beetle weight (3 pound) robots. One detail that makes combat robotics interesting is how different the problems are compared to the other competitions listed here. The other competitions often incentivize impressive but complex systems to complete tasks as efficiently as possible. However, in combat robotics, this is a robot that is going to be thrown every which way, making durability and build quality much more central factors than in other competitions, while reducing the prevalence of complex software solutions. This is an interesting challenge and may make for a more interesting sport to some people. 

Resources:
[IRCL website](https://ircl-io.github.io/)
[IRCL discord](https://discord.gg/xeQGwyJaTM)

In the discord, you may be able to find assistance for tasks like machining.
